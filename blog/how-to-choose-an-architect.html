How to Choose an Architect in Vizag or Hyderabad

What to check and ask before you sign anything

Hiring an Architect By Sai Nirmaan Architects 7 min read

Most people choose an architect the way they choose a restaurant: they look at photographs. It is a reasonable start and a poor place to finish. A portfolio shows what a practice has drawn. It does not show whether the drawings were built as drawn, who did the work, or what it was like to be the client.

We are an architecture practice, so we are not neutral here. We have still sat on the other side of this conversation often enough to know which questions help. Here are the ones we would ask in your place.

Check the registration first

In India the title "architect" is protected. Under the Architects Act, 1972, a person has to be registered with the Council of Architecture to practise as an architect and use the title. Ask for the registration number and look it up on the Council's website. It takes five minutes and filters out a surprising number of people who call themselves architects and are not.

Membership of the Indian Institute of Architects is a different thing. It is a professional association and joining is voluntary. It says something about how a practice engages with the profession, but it is not a licence, and it should not be accepted in place of registration.

Ask what has been built

Renderings are easy to make beautiful. Ask which images in the portfolio are finished buildings and which are proposals, and expect a straight answer. A good practice has both and will not blur them. On our own portfolio some projects are marked as design proposals for exactly this reason.

Better still, ask to visit one finished project, or to speak to the owner of one. People who have lived with a building for two or three years will tell you things no brochure will: whether the roof leaked in the first monsoon, whether the architect came back to fix it, whether the final cost matched the early talk.

Find out who will do the work

In a larger practice the person you meet in the first meeting is often not the person drawing your house. That is not automatically a problem, since good practices have good teams. It becomes one when you only find out afterwards.

"A portfolio shows what a practice has drawn. It does not show whether the drawings were built as drawn."

Ask who will design your project, who will prepare the working drawings, and who will visit the site and how often. If the answer to the last question is vague, take note. Most of the real decisions on a building are made on site, in conversations with a mason or a contractor, and they need someone with authority standing there.

Understand what the fee covers

Architects charge in a few different ways. Some take a percentage of the construction cost, some charge a rate per square foot, and some quote a fixed sum for defined stages. None of these is better than the others. What matters is that the arrangement is written down and tied to specific work.

Ask exactly what is included. Concept design, drawings for statutory approval, detailed working drawings, coordination with the structural and services engineers, site supervision and interior design are separate pieces of work, and a fee that looks low often covers only the first two. The gap shows up later, when you find that nobody is being paid to visit the site.

Ask about approvals in your city

Building permission runs through a different authority in each city, and the forms, rules and timelines are not the same. A practice that has taken plans through sanction in your area will know what the reviewing officers look for and where plans usually get stuck. One that has not may be perfectly capable, but you will be paying for its learning.

Treat any firm promise about how long approval will take with caution. The timeline belongs to the authority, not the architect. A sensible answer is a realistic range and an explanation of what could stretch it.

Say your budget out loud

Many clients hold their budget back in the first meeting, in case it gets used against them. It works the other way. An architect who knows the number can design to it from the first sketch. One who does not will design something, and you will find out the cost gap at tender stage, when changes are most expensive.

This is also why we took the budget field off our contact form. It is a poor way to have the conversation. We would rather you tell us in person, after we have seen the plot and understood what you need.

Warning signs

A few things should slow you down. A practice that cannot show finished work, or will not name a client you can speak to. A fee with no written scope behind it. Pressure to sign before you have seen a sample drawing set or a draft agreement. An offer of free design that is really a way of selling you construction. A guarantee on a statutory approval date.

None of these proves bad faith on its own. Together they tell you the practice is optimising for getting hired rather than for building your project well.

What a good first meeting looks like

A good first meeting is mostly the architect asking questions. How many people live in the house, and how does the household actually move through the day? What did you like and dislike in the last place? Which parts of the plot do you already love? If the first hour is spent on the architect's own work, it is a sales call.

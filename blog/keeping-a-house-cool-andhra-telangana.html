Keeping a House Cool in Andhra and Telangana

What lowers indoor heat, roughly in the order it pays off

Climate and Comfort By Sai Nirmaan Architects 7 min read

Air conditioning treats the symptom. The heat in a house in Visakhapatnam or Hyderabad comes in through a handful of places, and if you deal with those during design, the AC runs less and the rooms are bearable on the days it is off. If you leave them alone, you buy a bigger unit and pay for it every month.

None of what follows removes the need for cooling in the worst weeks of May. It makes the house start from a lower temperature, which is a different and better position.

Start with the roof

The terrace takes sun from morning to evening, and a concrete slab stores that heat and gives it back after dark. This is why the top-floor flat or the room under the terrace is the hottest one in the building, and why people there complain at ten at night when everywhere else has cooled down.

There are a few ways to deal with it. A reflective finish on the roof sends a lot of the sunlight away before it enters the slab, and the old practice of laying broken-tile mosaic over a waterproofing layer works on the same principle. A layer of insulation under the finish slows the heat further. Shading the terrace itself, with a pergola or with solar panels raised on a frame, keeps the slab out of direct sun altogether. A planted roof helps too, but only if the waterproofing underneath is done properly, because a leak under soil is miserable to trace.

If your budget allows one intervention above the ordinary, make it this one.

Treat the west wall as a problem

The afternoon sun is low, strong and arrives at the hottest part of the day. A west-facing wall full of glass takes the full force of it. We wrote about this in what changes between Vizag and Hyderabad, and it applies in both cities.

The simplest response is to keep openings on the west small, and to put rooms that are used briefly on that side: staircase, store, utility, bathrooms. They act as a buffer between the sun and the rooms where people live. Where a west window is unavoidable, shade it from outside, because shading that sits inside the glass lets the heat in first. Trees planted on the west side do a quiet and useful job, and cost almost nothing.

Choose the wall material with some thought

Walls built of different materials let heat through at different rates. Autoclaved aerated concrete blocks, usually called AAC, conduct less heat than conventional brick and are lighter, which is why many new houses use them. They need the right mortar and plaster, and fixing heavy items to them needs planning, so they are not a thing to swap in at the last minute.

A double wall with an air gap between the skins is another proven approach, particularly on the west and south faces. It costs more in floor area and material, so it is worth using where the sun is worst and skipping elsewhere.

Shade the glass from the outside

Glass is where the sun enters. A horizontal projection above a window, the chajja that older Indian houses had for good reason, blocks the high midday sun. The sun at the beginning and end of the day is low, and a projection does little against it, so those sides need vertical fins, louvres or a screen. A perforated jaali panel does both jobs and also gives privacy and a pleasant pattern of light.

Pale external colours reflect more heat than dark ones. A dark colour on a west wall looks handsome in a rendering and radiates heat for hours after sunset.

Let air move, with the climate in mind

The two cities need different habits here. Visakhapatnam is humid, so moving air matters a great deal: sweat does not evaporate easily, and a still room feels worse than the thermometer says. Windows opposite each other, so a breeze can cross the room, and high-level openings that let hot air escape, make a real difference.

Hyderabad is drier, and the useful habit is different. Open the house in the cooler hours of night and early morning so the structure loses heat, then close the windows and curtains through the day to keep it out. A house designed so this is easy to do, with safe night-time openings and good shutters, stays noticeably calmer than one built on the assumption that windows are always open.

Courtyards, trees and water

A small courtyard with planting gives a house a cooler air pocket and a way for hot air to rise out of the plan. Trees shade walls and paving and cool the air around them through the water they release. They take years to mature, which is a reason to plant on the day you build, not after.

These are helpful and modest. Do not expect a pond or a few pots to cool a house on their own.

Decide early

Almost everything above is cheap at the design stage and expensive afterwards. A different roof finish costs a little more at the start. Rebuilding a west wall, or adding shading to a finished facade, costs far more and often looks added on. The same is true of the plan itself: which rooms sit where decides how hot they get, and moving rooms after the walls are up is rarely worth it.

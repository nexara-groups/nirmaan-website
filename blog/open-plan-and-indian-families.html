Open Plan Homes and How Indian Families Actually Live

The layout everyone asks for, and the four things it tends to break

Interior Design By Sai Nirmaan Architects 7 min read

Open plan comes up in almost every residential brief we take. Clients have seen it in apartments, in magazines and on Instagram, and they want the kitchen, dining and living to run together as one space.

We build it often and it works well in the right house. It also produces more post-occupancy complaints than any other layout decision we deal with, and the complaints are remarkably consistent. They are worth knowing before the walls are left out rather than after.

The smell problem is not a small one

This is the first complaint and the loudest. Indian cooking produces a lot of airborne oil and a lot of smell. Tadka is a small controlled explosion of aromatics. Deep frying fills a room in minutes.

In a closed kitchen with a door, that stays mostly where it belongs. In an open plan, it reaches the sofa, the curtains, the upholstery and the clothes hanging in a bedroom down the hall. Families who cook Indian food daily, which is most families, notice this within the first month.

A good extraction system helps and does not solve it. The usual answer that does work is a hybrid: keep the visual openness, but give the heavy cooking its own enclosed space. A utility or wet kitchen behind the show kitchen is standard in better apartment plans for exactly this reason, and it is worth the floor area in an independent house too. The open kitchen handles tea, breakfast, assembly and conversation. The closed one handles the frying.

Some clients resist this because it sounds like two kitchens and therefore like expense. In practice the second one is small, unglamorous and the thing they end up most grateful for.

Sound travels further than people expect

Walls do more than divide space. They stop noise, and an open plan removes that quietly.

A pressure cooker whistling while someone is on a work call. The television at the volume a grandparent needs it. Children doing homework at the dining table while the kitchen is busy. In a house with three generations, which is a large share of what we design in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, these overlaps happen every day rather than occasionally.

The fix is not to abandon the open plan. It is to make sure the house has at least one properly enclosed room that is not a bedroom, and to think about where it sits. A study, a small sitting room, a den off the main space. Somewhere a door can be shut without someone having to go to bed to get quiet.

Guests change the rules

Indian households receive visitors who are not close friends. Relatives arrive unannounced. Neighbours come by. Colleagues, the broker, a priest, the extended family during a function.

"The version of open plan that survives contact with an Indian household is never a pure one."

An open plan puts all of this in the same volume as the family's ordinary life. When guests are in the living area, the kitchen is on display, the dining table has whatever is on it, and anyone passing between bedrooms walks through the middle of the gathering.

Families who entertain formally often want a separate reception space, and in that case a fully open plan is the wrong instrument. Where the budget or the plot will not allow a separate room, the layout can still be arranged so the circulation to the bedrooms does not cut across the seating, and so the kitchen is not the first thing a visitor sees. That is a planning decision, and it has to be made at the plan stage.

The privacy question nobody raises early

Open plan assumes the household is comfortable being visible to each other most of the time. Many are. Some are not, and it is not always the people you expect.

A working daughter-in-law taking a call. A teenager who wants to not be observed. An older family member who finds constant activity tiring and does not want to say so. These do not come up in a first meeting, because they feel too small to mention and sometimes too personal.

We have learned to ask directly, and the answers change plans. One household wanted the openness everywhere except a sightline from the kitchen to the main seating, because the person cooking did not want to be watched. That is one wall, placed deliberately, and it made the difference between a house they liked and a house they loved.

What usually works

The version of open plan that survives contact with an Indian household tends to look like this. Living and dining run together, because that genuinely helps and causes few problems. The kitchen opens to the dining visually, with a counter or a wide opening rather than a missing wall, and the serious cooking happens in an enclosed utility behind it. There is one more room with a door that is not a bedroom. Circulation to the private areas does not pass through the middle of the social space.

That is not a pure open plan and it does not photograph quite as cleanly. It is lived in more comfortably, which is the only test that matters after the first year.

Ask the right question

When a client asks for open plan, the useful follow-up is not how open, but what they are actually after. Usually it is one of three things: more daylight, a space where the family is together rather than scattered, or a house that does not feel cramped.

Each of those has several solutions and removing walls is only one of them. Sometimes it is the right one. Often a wider opening, a better window, a level change or a different furniture arrangement gets the feeling they want while keeping the separation they will need in year two.

Why We Walk the Site Before We Draw Anything

What an hour on the plot tells you that a survey drawing cannot

Practice By Sai Nirmaan Architects 6 min read

A client sends us a survey drawing, a plot number and a wish list, and asks how soon they can see a plan. It is a fair question and the honest answer disappoints some people: not until we have stood on the plot.

This sounds like an affectation. It is not. An hour on site changes the design more than a week at a desk, and the things it changes are the ones that are expensive to fix later.

What a survey drawing leaves out

A survey gives you boundaries, dimensions, levels and maybe a few existing features. Everything it gives you is accurate and none of it is the whole picture.

It will not tell you that the neighbouring plot has a three-storey wall right on the boundary, so the windows you were going to put on that side will look at render. It will not tell you that the approach road sits a metre below the plot, which changes the entrance, the ramp and the drainage. It will not tell you that there is a well-grown tamarind tree in the north-east corner that the family has been attached to for thirty years and would quietly like to keep.

It will not tell you that the site is directly under an approach path, or beside a function hall that runs loudspeakers until midnight twice a week, or next to a temple whose morning bells reach exactly the corner where you were planning the main bedroom.

Where the sun actually lands

Orientation looks like a drawing problem. North is marked on the survey. You can calculate the sun path for any latitude and any date without leaving the office.

What you cannot calculate is obstruction. A six-storey apartment block to the south-west removes the problem you were designing against and creates a different one. A clear western edge with nothing beyond it for two hundred metres means the afternoon sun arrives at full strength and will do so until somebody builds there, which they eventually will.

That last point matters more than people expect. Part of reading a site is reading what is going to happen around it. An empty neighbouring plot in a developing area of Vizag or Hyderabad is not permanent open space. It is a building that has not started yet. Designing your best views and your largest openings towards it is a decision that ages badly.

Water goes somewhere

On a flat survey drawing, water is invisible. On site it is often obvious, especially if you visit after rain.

"An empty neighbouring plot is not permanent open space. It is a building that has not started yet."

You can see where the ground is soft, where previous runoff has cut channels, where the neighbour's downpipe discharges, and whether the plot sits high or low relative to everything around it. A plot that collects water from three adjoining sites needs a different ground-floor level and a different drainage strategy, and finding that out during the monsoon after the family has moved in is a bad way to learn it.

We try to see a site twice when the programme allows, once in dry conditions and once after rain. It is not always possible. When it is, it has saved clients real money.

Talking to whoever is there

The watchman, the neighbour, the local mason, the man running the shop at the corner. These conversations are unscientific and they are consistently useful.

People who live beside a plot know things that are in no document: which way the wind comes in summer, whether the road floods, how reliable the water supply is on that street, what was on the site before, which contractor built the house opposite and whether it went well. We have had foundation problems predicted by a neighbour months before a soil report confirmed them.

The things families only say on site

This is the part that surprises people. Clients tell you different things when they are standing on their land.

In a meeting room, a brief is a list of rooms and a budget. On site, a client points at a corner and says that is where their mother will sit in the mornings. They mention that they want to see the gate from the kitchen because of the children. They say they do not want the staircase visible from the entrance, something they had not thought to write down.

A plot has a way of making people specific. Half the useful brief arrives in that hour, and most of it is the half that decides whether the family will like living there.

What it costs to skip

You can design from a survey drawing. Plenty of work gets done that way, particularly when the architect is in another city and the plot is somewhere inconvenient.

The result is usually a competent building with a few avoidable problems. A main bedroom facing a blank wall. A living room that takes the full western sun. A level difference discovered during excavation that adds cost nobody had allowed for. Each of these is survivable and each one is permanent.

An hour on the plot, early, costs a morning. We have yet to find a cheaper piece of design work.

Have a plot and no drawings yet?

That is the right time to talk to us. We will walk the site with you before anything is designed.

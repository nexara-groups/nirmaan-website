Working With Vastu Without Wrecking the Plan

How we reconcile traditional guidance with a workable modern house

Residential Design By Sai Nirmaan Architects 7 min read

Almost every residential enquiry we take in Visakhapatnam or Hyderabad arrives with a Vastu consultant already attached. Sometimes it is a family priest, sometimes a professional with a compass and a printed report. The client is usually a little apprehensive when they mention it, as though they expect the architect to sigh.

We do not sigh. In fifteen years of residential work across Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, we have found that the fight between Vastu and good planning is mostly imaginary. The real conflicts are few, and they are usually solvable. The trouble starts when nobody does the reconciling early enough.

Where Vastu and climate agree

A large part of classical Vastu is orientation advice written for this climate, long before anyone had a load calculation or a sun-path diagram. Kitchens to the south-east. Heavy mass and storage to the south and west. Openings and lighter rooms to the north and east.

Read that as building science and it holds up well. In coastal Andhra and across the Deccan, the south-west wall takes the worst of the afternoon sun. Putting thick walls, wardrobes and utility against it is exactly what a thermal engineer would tell you to do. North light is steady and does not overheat a room, which is why it suits bedrooms and study spaces. The south-east corner catches morning sun and prevailing breeze, which is useful in a kitchen that would otherwise trap heat and smell.

So when a client says the kitchen must go south-east, we rarely argue. It is where we would have put it anyway.

The conflicts that are real

Two situations genuinely create tension, and both come down to the plot rather than the beliefs.

The first is a plot that faces the wrong way. A south-facing or west-facing site in a city grid is not a problem a drawing can wish away. Many families arrive convinced that a south-facing plot is a defect they will have to live with. In practice the entrance position within that façade, the placement of the compound gate, and the internal sequence of rooms can satisfy most consultants without distorting the plan. The orientation of the plot and the orientation of the entrance are not the same thing, and a lot of anxiety dissolves once that distinction is made.

The second is a narrow urban plot, which is most of what gets built in Vizag and Hyderabad now. On a 30 by 50 site, Vastu prescriptions about leaving open space to the north and east collide directly with setback rules and with the simple need for rooms that are wide enough to use. Something has to give. This is where a consultant who works only from a checklist and an architect who works only from a brief will produce two incompatible documents, and the family will be caught between them.

Get everyone in the room at the start

The single change that removes most of this friction costs nothing. Bring the Vastu consultant into the first design conversation, not the fourth.

"Constraints known at the beginning are design inputs. The same constraints discovered at the end are damage."

What usually happens instead: we develop a plan over several weeks, the family is happy with it, the consultant sees it for the first time at that stage, and a list of objections comes back. Now every fix is a retrofit. Moving a staircase at that point ripples through the structure, the services and the elevation. Everybody gets frustrated, the family feels caught in the middle, and the house gets worse rather than better, because the changes are patches rather than decisions.

When the consultant is present at the start, their requirements become part of the brief, sitting alongside the budget, the family size and the setbacks. Constraints known at the beginning are just design inputs. Constraints discovered at the end are damage.

What we will push back on

We are not neutral about everything. There are a few recommendations we will question, and we tell clients this openly before we start.

We will argue against anything that makes a room genuinely unlivable: a bedroom with no cross ventilation in a city where summer runs nine months, a kitchen with no exhaust path, a toilet that can only be reached through a living room. We will also argue when a prescription forces a structurally wasteful solution, such as a column grid that fights the spans and adds cost to no benefit.

In our experience most consultants are reasonable when the problem is explained in terms of the family's comfort rather than in terms of the architect's preference. Many have worked on hundreds of houses and know perfectly well where the guidance can flex. The ones who will not flex at all are rare, and when that happens the family has to decide which advisor they trust. That is their decision to make, not ours, and we say so.

A note on resale

There is a practical argument that has nothing to do with belief. In Visakhapatnam and Hyderabad, a house that violates well-known Vastu principles is harder to sell. Buyers walk away, or they discount. A family who does not personally care about Vastu may still care about that, and it is worth raising early rather than discovering it a decade later.

The useful question

The question we ask clients is not whether they believe in Vastu. That is personal and it is not our business. The question is how much weight they want it to carry when it competes with daylight, ventilation, cost or the size of a room.

Some families answer that Vastu wins every time. Some say it matters for the kitchen and the main entrance and nowhere else. Both answers are workable. What does not work is leaving the question unanswered until the drawings are done, because then the answer gets made by whoever shouts last.

Planning a home in Vizag or Hyderabad?

We work with your Vastu consultant from the first sketch, so the plan and the guidance are settled together rather than fought over later.
